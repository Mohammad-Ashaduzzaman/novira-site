What happens when nobody can agree what happened

Almost every expensive problem in the physical economy traces back to a single failure — and it isn't the failure you'd expect.

Kamal Anandpuri · Founder & CEO9 min read

Picture the moment a shipment arrives and something is wrong. The cartons are crushed, or the temperature log looks off, or the count doesn't match. What happens next is the same in almost every industry: the room divides into people who each believe a different version of events, and none of them can prove the one they believe.

The shipper is sure it left in perfect condition. The carrier is sure nothing happened on their watch. The receiver is sure it arrived damaged. The insurer is sure of nothing and suspicious of everyone. Each account is sincere. Each is unprovable. And so begins the expensive part — not the damage itself, but the argument about the damage.

We started Novira because we became convinced that this moment — the moment nobody can agree what happened — is one of the largest and least-examined sources of cost in the physical economy. It hides inside line items called "claims," "disputes," "shrinkage," and "write-offs," but underneath all of them is the same root cause.

The real problem isn't damage. It's ambiguity.

Damage, loss, delay — these are normal. Things go wrong in the physical world; everyone budgets for that. What no one budgets for is how much it costs to not know, defensibly, what actually went wrong.

Ambiguity is what turns a small loss into a long one. A damaged pallet is a number. A damaged pallet that four parties disagree about is weeks of emails, a stalled invoice, a chargeback, a strained relationship, and eventually a settlement that satisfies no one — paid not because anyone established the truth, but because everyone got tired of arguing. The damage was the cheap part.

When no one can prove what happened, the loss doesn't go to whoever caused it. It goes to whoever has the least leverage.

That last point is the one that should bother anyone who cares about fairness as much as cost. In the absence of proof, outcomes aren't decided by what occurred. They're decided by who has more power in the relationship, more lawyers, more patience. The truth doesn't win, because the truth was never actually available.

Why we never solved this before

The strange thing is that we've spent two decades pouring sensors into the physical world. We can see almost everything now. So why can't we prove anything?

Because seeing and proving are different problems, and we only solved the first one. A sensor reading is a claim: it says "this is what I measured." But a claim from a device you control, transmitted through systems you control, stored in a database you can edit, is exactly as persuasive to a skeptical counterparty as your own word — which is to say, not very. The data was built to be looked at by you, not trusted by someone who'd rather not believe it.

So the data piles up, and the disputes continue anyway, and everyone quietly accepts that "what happened" is fundamentally unknowable once the goods have moved. We decided that assumption was wrong — and worth a company.

What it takes to actually settle it

To make a physical event impossible to argue with, a few things have to be true at once. The event has to be captured at the source, as it happens. It has to be verified — confirmed genuine, unaltered, and tied to the right thing — so it can't be waved away as a glitch or a fabrication. It has to be sealed into a record that can't be quietly revised later. And it has to be shareable with everyone who has a stake, so they're all looking at one truth instead of three stories.

Do that, and the moment that used to start an argument ends one instead. The verified record arrives, and there's nothing to dispute — not because anyone was forced to agree, but because the facts are finally standing in the room with them. From there, the useful things become possible: protection can act on the verified event automatically, and a system can begin to learn which situations are likely to go wrong before they do.

That chain — capture, verify, trust, share, act, learn — isn't a feature list. It's the answer to a single question we think the physical economy has been quietly losing money to for decades: what happens when nobody can agree what happened?

The answer, until now, was: the person with the least leverage pays. We built Novira to change the answer to: the truth does.

Key takeaways
  • The real cost of a physical-world failure is rarely the damage — it's the ambiguity about what happened.
  • When no one can prove events, outcomes are decided by leverage, not by truth.
  • Sensors gave us visibility, but visibility isn't proof; a reading you control persuades no skeptical counterparty.
  • Capture, verify, trust, share, act, learn — the chain exists to make a physical event impossible to argue with.

We don't sell certainty about the future. We give you the truth about what happened.
