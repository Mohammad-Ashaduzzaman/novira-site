The future of parametric insurance

Parametric insurance has always had one weak point. It isn't the model — it's the trigger.

Kamal Anandpuri · Founder & CEO8 min read

Parametric insurance is a beautiful idea. Instead of paying against a loss you have to investigate and adjust, you pay against a defined event: a measurable thing happened, the conditions were met, the response follows automatically. No reconstruction, no lengthy claim, no argument. Fast, clean, and cheap to administer.

It works wonderfully — wherever the trigger is something no one can dispute. A hurricane's wind speed at a weather station. An earthquake's magnitude. A flight's recorded delay. The reason parametric products thrive in those domains is that the trigger is measured by a trusted, independent source. The whole model rests on one assumption: everyone agrees the event happened.

The trigger problem

Now try to extend parametric coverage to the things that actually move through the physical economy — a shipment, a piece of equipment, a perishable cargo. The model immediately runs into its weak point. The trigger for these events isn't measured by an independent weather station. It's measured by a device that sits with the insured's goods, on the insured's side of the transaction.

That's a problem. If the payout fires automatically when a sensor says a threshold was crossed, then the trustworthiness of the entire product depends on the trustworthiness of that sensor reading — and a reading from a party with an interest in the payout is exactly the kind of trigger an insurer can't build a product on. The thing that makes parametric work in catastrophe lines, an undisputed trigger, is missing.

Parametric insurance doesn't have a modeling problem. It has a trust problem at the trigger.

This is why parametric products remain rare for granular, asset-level risks despite obvious demand. The economics are appealing. The model is sound. But the trigger can't be trusted, so the product can't be written — or it gets written with so much manual verification bolted on that it stops being parametric at all.

Verification is the missing ingredient

The unlock isn't a better model or more data. It's a trigger that can be trusted by both sides without either having to take the other's word for it. That requires the event itself to be verified at the source — confirmed genuine, unaltered, and tied to the right asset — and sealed into a record neither party can quietly revise.

Make the trigger verifiable, and the parametric model suddenly extends to everything it couldn't reach before. A verified temperature excursion can be a trigger. A verified shock event can be a trigger. A verified deviation from an approved route can be a trigger. Not because the insured says so, and not because the insurer eventually accepts it after investigation, but because the event is established as fact the moment it occurs.

What this makes possible

An insurer building on verified events can write parametric products for risks that were previously uninsurable in that form. When a verified event meets the policy's defined conditions, the response can initiate in the moment — the workflow activating automatically, with benefit delivery handled alongside the insured. Settlement compresses from weeks to near-immediate, because there was never a reconstruction to perform. Administrative cost falls. Fraud has little room, because the trigger isn't a claim to be tested — it's a verified fact.

And underwriting changes too. Pricing parametric risk has always been hard partly because the historical data is self-reported and noisy. Build the book on verified events and the risk models sharpen, because they're learning from what genuinely happened rather than what was claimed.

I think the next decade of insurance innovation in the physical economy runs through this single point. Not better catastrophe models — those are mature. The frontier is granular, asset-level parametric coverage for shipments, equipment, and goods in motion. That frontier has been blocked by one thing: the trigger couldn't be trusted. Make the physical event verifiable, and the block is gone.

Parametric insurance has been waiting for a trustworthy trigger. That's the part we're building.

Key takeaways
  • Parametric insurance works wherever the trigger is an undisputed, independently measured event.
  • For asset-level risks, the trigger sits on the insured's side — so it can't be trusted, and the product can't be written.
  • The unlock isn't a better model; it's a verifiable trigger both sides can trust without taking each other's word.
  • Verified events make granular parametric coverage possible — faster settlement, less fraud, sharper underwriting.

You can't price what you can't trust. The verifiable event is the trigger parametric insurance was missing.
