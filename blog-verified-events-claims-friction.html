How verified events reduce claims friction

Most of what makes a claim slow, costly, and contentious comes down to one thing: the proof shows up late, if at all.

Novira Intelligence Team7 min read

Every claim is, at its heart, a question: did the thing that's claimed to have happened actually happen, the way it's described? Everything that makes claims expensive — the investigation, the back-and-forth, the reserving, the fraud exposure, the time-to-settle — flows from how hard that question is to answer.

And today, for most physical-world claims, it's very hard to answer. The event being claimed already happened, somewhere the insurer wasn't, captured by instruments the insurer doesn't control, described by a party with an interest in the outcome. So the claim enters a process built to compensate for the absence of trustworthy proof. That process is the friction.

Friction is the cost of reconstructing the past

When a loss can't be observed directly, it has to be reconstructed. Adjusters gather statements, photos, estimates, and whatever telemetry exists, then assemble a plausible account of an event no one can replay. Reconstruction is slow, it's labor-intensive, and it's inherently contestable — two reasonable people can build two different accounts from the same fragments.

That contestability is where friction lives. It's why valid claims wait while questionable ones get scrutinized, why reserves are set conservatively against uncertainty, and why fraud finds room to operate in the gap between what's claimed and what's provable.

Ambiguity is the most expensive thing in the claim — slower than any process step and costlier than any single payout.

What a verified event changes

A verified event collapses the reconstruction problem. When the insured asset carries a device that captures the conditions that matter, and each significant event is verified at the source — confirmed genuine, unaltered, and tied to the right asset — the claim doesn't arrive as a story to be investigated. It arrives with its proof attached.

The adjuster isn't reconstructing what might have happened; they're reading what's established to have happened. The insured isn't trying to convince a skeptic; they're pointing at a record the skeptic also trusts. The same fact sits in front of both parties, which means there's far less to negotiate and far less room for either error or fraud.

Where the friction goes

Remove the ambiguity and the downstream effects compound. Investigation shrinks, because there's less to investigate when the event is already established. Settlement accelerates, because both sides are working from the same evidence. Fraud contracts, because the gap it exploits — the space between claimed and provable — has largely closed. And reserving sharpens, because risk can be priced against verified history rather than noisy, self-reported data.

None of this requires the insurer to be more trusting or the insured to be more honest. It requires the event itself to be verifiable — so that trust is a property of the record, not of the relationship.

The shape of a lower-friction claim

The endpoint is a claim that validates almost as fast as it's filed. Where a verified event meets the policy's terms, the response can even begin automatically — the workflow initiating in the moment, with benefit delivery handled alongside the insured rather than after weeks of review. The valid claim settles fast. The questionable one is caught early. And the loss ratio starts to reflect what genuinely happened, rather than what simply couldn't be disproven.

Claims will always be the core of the business. But the friction around them — the slow, costly, contentious part — turns out to be a proof problem. Solve the proof, and the friction has nowhere left to hide.

Key takeaways
  • Claims friction is the cost of reconstructing an event no one can replay.
  • Ambiguity — the gap between what's claimed and what's provable — is where fraud and delay live.
  • A verified event arrives with its proof attached, so there's little left to investigate or negotiate.
  • Trust becomes a property of the record, not the relationship — sharpening settlement, fraud control, and reserving.
